Model Deployment Encode, Prefill and Decode Benchmark

A live run on 4 × NVIDIA L40S compared three serving shapes for Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct: aggregated, prefill/decode and encode/prefill/decode (EPD). Three workloads exercise them: a prompt sweep from 1k to 8k tokens, a mixed stream of heavy-vision and small requests, and one image queried repeatedly. Model Deployment Encode, Prefill and Decode describes the EPD topology.

EPD is not faster everywhere. At 1k and 2k tokens the aggregated arm has the lowest time to first token (TTFT), because two network hops cost more than a short vision pass. At 4k and 8k under concurrency the order reverses. At 8k with four requests in flight, EPD has a TTFT p50 of 241.8 ms, against 697.9 ms for prefill/decode (2.89×) and 757.1 ms for aggregated (3.13×).

At that point EPD also reaches 128.24 tok/s, 13.3% above prefill/decode and 5.7% above aggregated. At 8k with eight in flight its inter-token latency (ITL) p99 is 52.72 ms, while aggregated degrades to 199.34 ms.

Under mixed traffic the advantage depends on the stream. EPD protects the small requests (226.5 ms TTFT p50, against 315.1 ms and 388.3 ms) but is the slowest arm for the heavy-vision requests (5017.0 ms, against 2854.0 ms for aggregated). On a repeated 4k image EPD wins outright: 356.0 ms TTFT p50 at four in flight (2.22× faster than prefill/decode) and 31.9% higher output throughput at eight.

The three arms

Each arm is one ModelDeployment with one replica per role and one accelerator per replica. The arms differ in topology and in how many cards they use.

Arm Roles Cards KV connector Encoder connector
Aggregated server 1 none none
Prefill/Decode prefill, decode 2 MooncakeConnector over tcp none
EPD encode, prefill, decode 3 MooncakeConnector over tcp ECCPUConnector over NIXL

The aggregated arm needs no connector, because one instance holds the whole model. Prefill and decode hand KV blocks across two Pods, pinned to tcp because the nodes have no RDMA interface. EPD adds the encoder leg on top of the same KV transfer. See Encoder-cache connectors for what each connector moves.

The scheduler placed the roles on two nodes, node-a and node-b. In the EPD arm, encode and prefill shared node-a and decode ran on node-b — the weight-50 pair in Scheduling co-location . The other two arms landed as follows:

Arm Role Node
Prefill/Decode prefill node-b
Prefill/Decode decode node-a
Aggregated server node-b

Every arm ran the same weights, so the comparison is per arm, not per card: the aggregated arm used one card and EPD used three. --max-model-len is 16384 so that the 8k prompts are not truncated. The prefill/decode and aggregated arms also set --enable-scale-out, which their router needs to count tokens. EPD does not need it.

All three suites ran on every arm, and every request succeeded.

Suite 1 workload

Suite 1 holds the completion budget at max_tokens=128 and varies only the prompt. Qwen2.5-VL turns an S-square image into (S / 28)² visual tokens.

Context Image Visual tokens Text tokens Measured prompt tokens
1k 560 × 560 400 624 1072 to 1073
2k 840 × 840 900 1148 2101 to 2102
4k 1260 × 1260 2025 2071 4157 to 4158
8k 1792 × 1792 4096 4096 8281 to 8282

Each context runs at concurrency 1, 4 and 8, with 8, 12 and 24 requests.

Suite 2 workload

Suite 2 runs two streams against one arm at the same time. Four workers ask for 1k requests (560 × 560, 1073 prompt tokens, max_tokens=256). Four other workers send 4k heavy-vision bursts (a unique 1792 × 1792 PNG per request, 4523 prompt tokens, max_tokens=64). Each stream sends twelve requests, and no image repeats, so no cache can answer a burst.

Suite 3 workload

Suite 3 sends one shared 1792 × 1792 PNG (4527 prompt tokens, max_tokens=64) with a different question per request, at concurrency 4 and 8. This is the multi-turn shape: the image is fixed and the encoder’s output is worth keeping.

Method

Two warm-up requests preceded every Suite 1 and Suite 3 configuration and are excluded from the results. Each configuration ran once, so every number below is a single measurement. The arms ran one after another on a shared cluster, and the later arms had the warmer nodes. See What this run does not measure .

Time per output token barely separates the arms. TPOT p50 stays between 20.29 ms and 26.72 ms in all thirty-six cells, and output throughput follows concurrency, not topology: 1k climbs from about 45 tok/s at one request in flight to 291 tok/s at eight.

Time to first token is where the topology shows.

One request in flight. Aggregated is fastest at every context, and EPD beats prefill/decode at every one. A request pays a transfer hop for each connector before its first token, and at low concurrency nothing is waiting behind it. At 1k the EPD penalty is 81.3 ms, because the transfer costs more than a 400-token vision pass.

Eight requests in flight. The two disaggregated arms hold ITL p99 flat, while aggregated degrades with prompt length:

Concurrency 8 1k 2k 4k 8k
Aggregated 60.12 94.36 167.79 199.34
EPD 53.39 47.46 49.23 52.72
Prefill/Decode 65.57 47.18 47.81 51.19

In the aggregated arm a long prefill shares a card with the requests already generating tokens. Its 4k and 8k ITL p99 is 3.4× and 3.8× EPD’s. Prefill/Decode removes the same interference, so EPD and prefill/decode differ little on this metric.

TTFT p50 at eight in flight crosses over as the prompt grows. At 1k and 2k EPD is still behind aggregated. At 4k it is behind on TTFT p50 (959.4 ms against 644.9 ms) but well ahead on ITL p99 (49.23 ms against 167.79 ms). At 8k EPD wins both: TTFT p50 is 880.7 ms against 1243.5 ms (aggregated) and 1513.9 ms (prefill/decode).

The 8k cell at four in flight is the strongest result and the weakest measurement. EPD’s TTFT p50 of 241.8 ms is the lowest of all thirty-six cells, but its p95 is 2279.9 ms, worse than aggregated’s 1880.9 ms. With twelve requests, the p95 sits between the slowest two samples, so treat the median and the tail as separate readings.

Suite 2: mixed heavy-vision and concurrent decode

Two streams share one arm: four workers ask for 1k completions while four workers push 4k unique-image bursts through the same GPU. This test shows how isolating the encoder changes what each arm is good at.

Workload Arm Requests TTFT p50 (ms) TTFT p95 (ms) TPOT p50 (ms) TPOT p95 (ms) ITL p95 (ms) ITL p99 (ms) Output (tok/s) Throughput (req/s)
concurrent_decode_1k Aggregated 12/12 388.3 502.1 24.39 27.76 25.3 89.22 69.2 1.038
concurrent_decode_1k EPD 12/12 226.5 266.4 24.52 26.18 47.99 66.87 66.96 0.972
concurrent_decode_1k Prefill/Decode 12/12 315.1 380.2 24.37 25.58 46.02 55.66 63.12 0.879
heavy_vision_4k Aggregated 12/12 2854.0 5677.9 38.14 75.86 176.22 386.39 66.43 1.038
heavy_vision_4k EPD 12/12 5017.0 6964.2 27.33 39.96 50.39 53.42 62.19 0.972
heavy_vision_4k Prefill/Decode 12/12 6187.9 7977.2 28.57 38.55 50.15 63.61 56.23 0.879

Every arm completed both streams. The two streams share one batch window: 11.561 s for aggregated, 12.35 s for EPD and 13.658 s for prefill/decode. The per-stream throughput columns are two views of that one window.

The 1k stream prefers EPD. EPD’s TTFT p50 is 226.5 ms, against 315.1 ms for prefill/decode and 388.3 ms for aggregated, and its p95 is the lowest at 266.4 ms. In prefill/decode the prefill engine runs the 1792 × 1792 vision pass inline, so small prefills queue behind it. In EPD the encoder absorbs those passes on its own GPU. In aggregated, the same prefill lands in the engine that is also decoding.

The 4k stream prefers aggregated on first token and EPD on every token after it. Aggregated’s TTFT p50 of 2854.0 ms is 1.76× faster than EPD’s 5017.0 ms and 2.17× faster than prefill/decode’s 6187.9 ms, because no embedding crosses a network. After the first token the order inverts. Aggregated’s ITL p99 is 386.39 ms, against 53.42 ms for EPD (7.23× worse) and 63.61 ms for prefill/decode. Its TPOT p95 is 75.86 ms, against 39.96 ms and 38.55 ms.

Combined throughput over the window is 135.63 tok/s for aggregated, 129.15 tok/s for EPD and 119.35 tok/s for prefill/decode. EPD gives up about 4.8% of aggregate throughput to make the 1k stream’s TTFT 1.39× faster than prefill/decode and the 4k stream’s ITL p99 7.23× faster.

Suite 3: repeated-image encoder-cache reuse

One shared 1792 × 1792 image, a different question per request, at two concurrencies. The image is 4096 visual tokens of encoder work that a cache could answer once.

repeated_4k_c4 4 Aggregated 12/12 621.4 786.8 23.76 76.57 120.17 1.878
repeated_4k_c4 4 EPD 12/12 356.0 422.7 22.24 37.17 143.16 2.237
repeated_4k_c4 4 Prefill/Decode 12/12 790.6 848.9 22.25 28.61 116.51 1.82

repeated_4k_c8 8 Prefill/Decode 24/24 1242.6 1437.3 22.44 49.76 191.01 2.985

This is EPD’s clearest result, and the only suite it wins outright. At concurrency 4 its TTFT p50 of 356.0 ms is 2.22× faster than prefill/decode and 1.75× faster than aggregated, with the lowest p95 (422.7 ms). At concurrency 8 it reaches 251.92 tok/s, 31.9% above prefill/decode and 31.6% above aggregated, and TTFT p50 is about half (570.2 ms against 1242.6 ms and 1131.3 ms).

The cause is the encoder cache. Suite 2’s heavy-vision stream has the same 4096-token encoder input with a unique image per request, and there EPD was the slowest arm on first token. Here the image repeats and EPD is the fastest. ECCPUConnector can answer later requests from a cached embedding, while prefill/decode re-runs the vision pass inline and aggregated re-runs it on the card that is also decoding.

Prefill/decode has the lowest ITL p99 at concurrency 4 (28.61 ms). Once the embeddings are in hand, the extra encoder leg costs little.

Stage execution verification

A 200 does not show which engines ran, because a router that answered every request from one replica would return the same status and similar latency. After the run, each arm’s engines were read for the access line POST /v1/chat/completions. For the EPD decoder, the routing-proxy sidecar was read instead for processing multimodal items, because only the sidecar logs what arrived from the encoder.

Arm Stage Requests
EPD encode 264
EPD prefill 264
EPD decode 264
EPD decode sidecar 264 multimodal items
Prefill/Decode prefill 430
Prefill/Decode decode 264
Aggregated server 530

The harness issued 264 requests across the three suites. EPD shows three equal counts, so every multimodal request was encoded once, prefilled once and pulled by the decoder once.

The 430 and 530 counts exceed 264 because the marker also matches the router’s token-render call, POST /v1/chat/completions/render. The prefill/decode and aggregated arms have no encoder role, so their router counts tokens by calling the engine. The EPD arm uses the router’s built-in estimator, which makes no HTTP call. This is why only those two arms need --enable-scale-out.

The router’s prefix-aware decision also needs a prompt of at least one 16-token KV block. A shorter prompt is scheduled to the decoder alone. Keep probe prompts past about twenty tokens when you check stage counts through this router.

When EPD helps and when it does not

Use aggregated when prompts are short or concurrency is low. It has the lowest TTFT in every cell at concurrency 1, and at 1k and 2k under load, on one card instead of three. A vision pass of a few hundred tokens does not repay two extra network hops. Prefill/decode shows the same cost: its TTFT p50 at 1k and concurrency 1 is 198.6 ms, against 99.1 ms for aggregated.

Use prefill/decode when decode streams must stay smooth and no image repeats. It matches EPD on ITL p99 in Suite 1 at concurrency 8 and beats it in Suite 3 at concurrency 4, on two cards instead of three. Its weakness is first token on heavy vision: 6187.9 ms on the 4k stream, and 315.1 ms on the 1k stream that shares the arm with it. Both are worse than EPD.

Use EPD for long-prompt multimodal traffic under concurrency, or when the same image repeats. It is the only arm that wins on TTFT p50 and ITL p99 together, at 8k with concurrency 4 and 8 and across Suite 3. The encoder cache pays whenever an image repeats, as in multi-turn chat, and needs no concurrency to show a benefit.

Avoid EPD when every request carries a unique large image and first-token latency matters most. In Suite 2 it was 1.76× slower than aggregated on the heavy-vision stream.

Splitting vision onto its own card pays when the shared card would otherwise be busy: concurrency above one, and contexts of 4k and above. Below those thresholds, with no repeated image, the third role only moves embeddings that a single engine would have produced in place.

What this run does not measure

  • Repetition. One batch per configuration, 8 to 24 requests per cell. Treat tail differences below roughly 10% as unmeasured. Read the 8k TTFT p50 of 241.8 ms at concurrency 4 together with its 2279.9 ms p95.
  • Arm order. The aggregated arm was resident and warm while the EPD benchmark ran, and it was benchmarked while prefill/decode was still starting. Later arms had the warmer cluster.
  • Interconnect. Neither node has an RDMA interface, so the KV leg ran over tcp and the encoder leg over NIXL on shared memory. A 100 GbE or RDMA fabric would shrink the transfer penalties that decide the 1k and 2k columns.
  • Parallelism. TP 1 per role on one 7B model. A model that needs tensor parallelism changes what each role holds and what separating them buys.
  • Graph capture. --enforce-eager was set on every role. This removes CUDA graphs and lowers decode throughput relative to a default deployment.
  • Model coverage. One 7B vision-language model, on one engine (vLLM) and one platform (nvidia). The SGLang nvidia rows are verified functionally in the EPD Walkthrough . The ascend and amd rows of the admitted matrix are not verified on hardware.
  • Cache hit rate. Suite 3 shows what a repeated image does, not how the hit rate is computed or what invalidates an entry. The benefit assumes the same image arrives with the same preprocessed token prefix.
